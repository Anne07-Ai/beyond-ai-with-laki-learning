What are embeddings?

Illustrated learning guide · Beginner to AI engineer

How a search for a playful young cat can find an energetic kitten—even when the words are different.

One-minute takeaway: An embedding is a numerical representation produced by a learned model. It can represent a token, sentence, document or another kind of data. Suitable embeddings let a system compare related items mathematically. They capture useful patterns, not a perfect understanding of the world.

1. A vector is a list of numbers

A vector is an ordered list of numbers. For example, [0.2, −0.4, 0.7] has three components, so we call it a three-dimensional vector. Order matters: swapping components changes the vector.

An embedding uses a vector to represent an item in a learned space. The numbers are produced using model parameters learned during training. They are not a dictionary definition, a confidence score or an arbitrary identification number.

Imagine a map where related ideas tend to appear near each other. That is a useful starting picture. The actual relationships depend on the model, its training objective and the similarity measure we choose. See [1] and [2].

2. From text to a representation

In a typical language model, a tokenizer first turns text into token IDs. Each ID selects a learned input vector. The model then processes these vectors in context, producing representations that depend on surrounding tokens. For example, the representation of bank can differ between a river bank and a savings bank. [3]

For semantic search, a text embedding model produces a vector for a sentence or passage. Many encoders combine token-level representations through an operation called pooling, though architectures vary. A raw token embedding and a search-ready sentence embedding are different things. [4]

How does the model learn this? Training adjusts parameters using a task-specific loss. Word models can learn from neighbouring words; retrieval models can learn to score relevant query–passage pairs above unsuitable pairs. “Close” means useful under that training setup, not universally equivalent. [2] [4]

3. Kitten, cat and dog: a map of meaning

Kitten and cat share many associations. Dog shares others, such as being a pet. An animal-focused representation might place kitten and cat near each other, with dog somewhat further away and toaster elsewhere. This is an illustration, not a measured ranking: another model or context may organise them differently.

Do not read a component as “the cat number” or “the friendliness number”. Individual dimensions usually do not have clean, fixed human labels. Meaningful patterns are distributed across the representation.

4. Semantic search: meet Luna

You search an adoption website for “a playful young cat”. Luna's listing says “An energetic kitten looking for a home.” A suitable search model can connect those descriptions even without an exact phrase match. Keyword search remains useful for exact identifiers and names; many real systems combine lexical and vector retrieval.

  1. Prepare: encode each listing and store its vector with the original listing ID.
  2. Encode: create a query vector using the matching model configuration.
  3. Compare: score candidate vectors with the model's intended similarity measure.
  4. Return: retrieve the original listings, apply business rules and show relevant results.

Suppose a cat-shaped lamp also scores highly. It is related to cats, but it is not an adoptable animal. Restricting results to real adoption listings and checking metadata can prevent that mistake. A similarity score is not a probability that a result is correct.

5. Worked example: comparing two vectors

These are hand-chosen 2D teaching vectors, not real model outputs. Let the query be q = [1, 0], Luna's listing L = [0.8, 0.6], a dog listing D = [0.6, 0.8] and a toaster listing T = [0, 1]. All four have length 1.

Cosine similarity compares direction: divide the dot product by the product of vector lengths. For non-zero vectors:

cos(q, x) = (q · x) / (‖q‖ × ‖x‖)

For Luna: the dot product is 1 × 0.8 + 0 × 0.6 = 0.8. Her vector length is √(0.8² + 0.6²) = 1. Therefore cos(q, L) = 0.8 / (1 × 1) = 0.8.

Cosine ranges from −1 to 1 mathematically; higher means more aligned directions. It is undefined for a zero vector. A score of 0.8 does not mean “80% correct”. Dot product and distance measures are alternatives; choose the measure recommended for the model and index. For unit-normalised vectors, dot product equals cosine. [6]

6. Where embeddings help

  • Search: retrieve help articles even when the user's phrasing differs.
  • Recommendations: compare item or user representations to suggest relevant content. Additional ranking and business rules still matter.
  • Clustering: group related support tickets for analysis; humans should inspect the groups.
  • Duplicate detection: identify similar documents, then verify whether they really are duplicates.
  • Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG): retrieve relevant passages, then supply them to a generator as context. Retrieval is one component; embeddings do not themselves write or verify the answer. [7]

7. For curious engineers: make retrieval reliable

The following are practical design considerations, not universal settings. Validate them on your own data.

  • Chunking: split documents into meaningful passages that fit the encoder's input limit. Preserve document IDs, source locations and permission metadata. Avoid cutting a sentence away from the context it needs.
  • Model compatibility: record the model/version, dimensions, preprocessing, query/document instructions and normalisation. Equal dimensions alone do not make two embedding spaces compatible. Re-embed and rebuild or migrate the index when changing incompatible models.
  • Retrieval choices: small collections can use exact scoring. Approximate nearest-neighbour indexes trade some recall for speed and scale. Compare them with an exact baseline.
  • Hybrid and reranking: lexical retrieval can preserve exact terms; a reranker can evaluate the query and candidate text together. Measure whether extra latency earns better relevance.
  • Evaluation: build representative queries with relevance judgements. Track Recall@k for coverage, MRR for the first relevant result, nDCG for graded ranking, and p95 latency. Include ambiguous language, negation and domain vocabulary. Do not compare raw scores across models as if calibrated alike.
  • Access control: enforce tenant and document permissions in retrieval and before returning content. Similarity is not authorisation. Retrieve only from a user's permitted collection where possible.
  • Data handling: embeddings are not encryption or guaranteed anonymisation. Protect vectors and source text, restrict retention and avoid sending private content to an unapproved provider.
  • RAG safety: treat retrieved text as untrusted data. A highly ranked passage can still contain a prompt injection or false claim. Keep tool permissions and instructions separate from document content.

8. Common misunderstandings

  • “A token ID is an embedding.” No: the ID is an index; the embedding is a vector.
  • “All models share one map.” No: independently trained spaces are generally not directly comparable.
  • “More dimensions always means better.” No: task fit and measured relevance matter, alongside storage and latency.
  • “Similar means true.” No: contradictory statements can share vocabulary and subject matter.
  • “The 3D animation shows the model's actual brain.” No: it is an educational illustration.

Glossary — keep these handy

Vector
An ordered list of numbers.
Embedding
A learned numerical representation of an item.
Dimension
A component or coordinate in a vector.
Token ID
A vocabulary index assigned by a tokenizer.
Contextual representation
A representation influenced by surrounding input.
Pooling
Combining multiple representations into one, according to a model's design.
Semantic similarity
Relatedness of meaning estimated by a model and comparison method.
Cosine similarity
A comparison of the directions of non-zero vectors.
Vector index
A structure for finding nearby stored vectors.
Reranker
A model or method that reorders retrieved candidates.
Projection
A mapping to fewer dimensions that can lose or distort information.
RAG
Retrieval-augmented generation: retrieving material to support generation.

Interview practice — beginner to AI engineer

Practise answering aloud in your own words. Strong answers connect the definition to a practical consequence.

1. What is an embedding?

A vector representation produced using learned parameters. It encodes patterns useful for a task, such as comparing text descriptions.

2. How is a vector different from a token ID?

A token ID is a discrete vocabulary index. A vector has multiple numerical components; a learned lookup maps IDs to input embeddings.

3. What does embedding dimensionality mean?

It is the number of vector components. It affects storage and computation, but higher dimensionality alone does not guarantee better relevance.

4. How are token and sentence embeddings different?

A token embedding represents one token. A sentence embedding represents a sequence as a single vector, often using contextual processing and pooling. Search suitability depends on training.

5. What are contextual representations?

They depend on surrounding text. The same token can have different hidden representations in different sentences, unlike a fixed input lookup vector.

6. How does semantic search work?

Encode documents and the query into compatible spaces, compare vectors, and return the source items for promising matches. Apply permissions and relevance checks.

7. When is cosine useful, and what are its limits?

It compares vector direction independent of magnitude for non-zero vectors. It is not a calibrated correctness probability; use the model's intended measure.

8. Why use hybrid search?

Dense retrieval can capture related phrasing, while lexical search helps with exact terms such as product codes. Combine and evaluate them on representative queries rather than assuming either always wins.

9. How would you choose chunk sizes for RAG?

Respect the encoder limit, document structure and evidence boundaries. Evaluate candidate sizes and overlap against retrieval quality, cost and latency; no single chunk size fits every corpus.

10. How would you evaluate retrieval?

Use held-out queries with judged relevance. Measure Recall@k, MRR or nDCG as appropriate, inspect failure cases, and track latency and resource cost.

11. What changes when you switch embedding models?

Verify dimensions, prompts, normalisation and scoring. Usually re-embed the corpus for an incompatible model, build a separate index, evaluate it and cut over with rollback support.

12. How do exact and approximate vector search differ?

Exact search evaluates the eligible collection exhaustively. Approximate search uses an index to reduce work, potentially missing neighbours. Benchmark recall and latency against exact retrieval.

13. How would you secure a multi-tenant vector service?

Enforce identity and tenant/document permissions, protect vectors and source text, separate indexes or filter securely, and verify access again before returning content. Never treat similarity as authorisation.

14. Why can a good retrieval score still produce a bad answer?

The passage may be outdated, contradictory, irrelevant to a constraint or malicious. Inspect source quality and permissions, evaluate reranking, and verify generated claims against evidence.
